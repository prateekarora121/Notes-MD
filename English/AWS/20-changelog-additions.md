1. **ECS vs EKS vs Fargate vs Lambda for .NET Workloads** — the original notes compare Lambda-vs-ECS and EC2-vs-Fargate separately but never give a direct, decision-table answer to the standard "how do you choose compute for a .NET microservices migration" question, including the .NET Framework/Windows-container angle.
2. **Deploying .NET to AWS: Elastic Beanstalk vs ECS vs Lambda Custom Runtime** — Elastic Beanstalk was entirely absent from the notes despite being a legitimate, commonly-asked-about .NET deployment path.
3. **S3 Storage Classes & Lifecycle Policies** — S3 was not covered at all in the source notes; storage class trade-offs and lifecycle JSON are near-guaranteed interview material.
4. **EBS vs EFS vs S3** — basic storage-type comparison, also absent from the source.
5. **VPC Reference Architecture (diagram)** — the notes explained VPC/subnet/NAT concepts textually but never assembled them into the canonical multi-AZ 3-tier diagram interviewers expect you to be able to draw.
6. **RDS Multi-AZ vs Read Replicas vs Aurora** — RDS was not covered at all, despite being more common than DynamoDB for primary OLTP in most .NET shops; this fills a major gap and the very common Multi-AZ/read-replica confusion.

10. **Least Privilege & Permission Boundaries in Practice** — "least privilege" was stated as a principle throughout the notes without a concrete before/after example or an explanation of permission boundaries vs SCPs.
11. **CloudWatch vs X-Ray: Complementary, Not Competing** — X-Ray was mentioned only in one line; expanded into a proper comparison and .NET integration notes, since "when do you use CloudWatch vs X-Ray" is a standard observability interview question.
12. **Cost Optimization: Savings Plans, Reserved, Spot** — EC2 pricing models were listed but never contrasted for a purchasing-decision question; added a comparison table and a "three-tier" cost strategy talking point.
13. **AWS Well-Architected Framework — 6 Pillars** — completely absent from the source, despite being the standard framework senior/architect interviews use to structure "how would you evaluate this architecture" questions.

## Summary of [net-fundamentals] Additions (This Pass)

Sixth pass, scope deliberately narrow. The guide's entire Networking section treated CIDR notation as *given* — `10.0.0.0/16`, `10.0.1.0/24`, `0.0.0.0/0`, `x.x.x.x/32` were all used without explanation, even though subnet sizing, route tables, SG rules, peering and IP exhaustion all rest on that one concept. Two subsections were added at the **start** of the Networking section, so everything else can be read on top of them.

1. **[IP Addressing, CIDR & Subnetting — Ground Up](11-networking.md#ip-addressing-cidr--subnetting--ground-up)** — the 32-bit binary view and the network-vs-host split; CIDR's "how many bits are locked" model; `Total = 2^(32−n)` and the "smaller prefix = bigger network" rule; the nine legal subnet-mask octet values; a master CIDR/mask/size table with an AWS-usable column; **AWS's 5 reserved IPs per subnet** (`/28` = 11 usable, not 16) and its link to `awsvpc`/EKS per-task IPs; the **block-size trick** (`256 − mask octet`) for non-octet prefixes and "is this IP in that block?"; a worked 3-tier × 3-AZ subnetting example with a third-octet labelling convention; **longest prefix match** and the precedence of the `local` route; four special CIDRs; the **RFC 1918** ranges (including the `172.16.0.0/12` vs `/16` trap); and a 30-second cheat sheet.
2. **[Overlapping CIDRs — The Design Mistake You Cannot Undo](11-networking.md#overlapping-cidrs--the-design-mistake-you-cannot-undo)** — the concrete example that was previously missing. Two teams both accepted the console's default `10.0.0.0/16`, and two years later need private connectivity: **peering simply won't create**; **TGW lets the attachment succeed and then silently blackholes traffic**; and — the real killer — the VPC's **`local` route** means the packet never leaves the VPC at all, so it doesn't even appear as a `REJECT` in Flow Logs. Plus the **partial-overlap** case where longest-prefix match steals a VPC's own `/24` from it; an escape-hatch table (re-IP vs secondary CIDR vs **PrivateLink** vs private NAT GW + `100.64.0.0/10`) with PrivateLink as the recommended fix; a day-one allocation registry; and five prevention rules — including **giving the DR region its own block** and **Amazon VPC IPAM**.

Cross-links were added from the CIDR-block paragraph in [VPC, Subnets, NAT](11-networking.md#vpc-subnets-nat--complete-model), from constraint #1 in [VPC Peering](11-networking.md#vpc-peering), and from Transit Gateway (where a new ⚠️ bullet clarifies that TGW does *not* solve overlapping CIDRs — it fails silently rather than rejecting them upfront, which is worse, not better). ## Summary of [topology] Additions (This Pass)

Seventh pass, and the only one whose origin is not the interview syllabus. Every earlier pass asked *"what is missing versus a 2026 senior bar?"* This one asked a different question: **"what did an actual production stack need that this guide doesn't cover?"** The reference stack is a containerized Windows service on **ECS-on-EC2**, behind an **internal ALB**, inside a **VPC the application does not own** — a three-repo Terraform topology whose headline property is that *no repository creates the VPC, subnets, NAT gateway or internet gateway.* All of them discover the network fabric by tag.

That reframing is what produced the gaps: the syllabus teaches you **how to build a VPC**, while most enterprise work has you **consume** one somebody else built. All five additions live in a single grouped section at the end of [Networking](11-networking.md#real-world-topology-pass--against-a-production-ecs-on-ec2-stack), each explaining *why it was a gap* before covering the material, and each anchored to a reference-topology diagram reproduced at the top of the group.

**Tag note:** these headings are tagged **`[topology]`**, not `[gaps]`, to keep this pass individually greppable — `[gaps]` belongs to pass 2. The one exception is the group's opening prose, which refers to `[gaps]` sections when contrasting this pass's origin with theirs.

1. **[Gap 1 — AWS Client VPN](11-networking.md#aws-client-vpn-getting-individual-users-inside-the-vpc)** — the largest gap, because the guide gave Client VPN **one throwaway line** at the end of Hybrid Connectivity while the reference topology's ALB is internal-only, making Client VPN the *only* human path to the service. Added: the four objects (endpoint, target network association, authorization rule, its own route table) and how their names differ entirely from Site-to-Site VPN's CGW/VGW/tunnels; a diagram of the **two independent gates** a connection must clear — the **authorization rule** (fails silently, connection shows UP with nothing reachable) and the **target's security group** (fails as a timeout) — which is the debugging insight the document lacked; the three auth modes (mutual TLS/ACM, AD with group-scoped rules, SAML federation) and that mutual combines with either; **split-tunnel vs full-tunnel as a cost decision**, since the default full-tunnel routes all employee internet traffic through your NAT Gateway's per-GB processing; the permanent client-CIDR mistakes (no overlap, min `/22`, **immutable after creation**); the cost shape missing from the per-service charging table (per subnet-association-hour accruing with zero users, plus per client-connection-hour — an hourly floor like NAT Gateway's); and connection logging, the self-service portal, the Client Connect Lambda, and the "set DNS servers or private hosted zones won't resolve" trap.
2. **[Gap 2 — The network is inherited, not owned](11-networking.md#the-network-is-inherited-not-owned-shared-vpc--the-central-platform-model)** — previously **not a single word**, despite being the reference topology's headline finding. Added a three-model comparison (platform-owned VPC per account / **Shared VPC via RAM** / VPC-per-account + TGW) across boundary, IP efficiency, isolation, NAT cost and blast radius. RAM appeared in this guide only for TGW and IPAM sharing, so **subnet sharing** — its most impactful use, and the answer to "how do you conserve IP space and NAT cost across a multi-account estate?" — is now covered: what the owner keeps, what participants can and cannot do, that **cross-account SG referencing works inside a shared VPC** (without which you'd hardcode CIDRs), and that **same-AZ inter-account traffic is free**, often a bigger win than the IP saving. Plus the survival notes for the far more common platform-owned model: the contract is **tags, not ARNs**; `ignore_tags` and why every `plan` otherwise fights platform tags forever; NAT existing only implicitly, so a broken egress isn't fixable in your repo; inheriting subnet-sizing pain you don't control; and that flat subnets plus an all-ports self-ingress fleet SG make the isolation **accepted, not achieved** — which belongs in writing as a risk. Closes with what to demand from a platform team, which is itself a senior-level answer.
3. **[Gap 3 — Internal vs internet-facing ALB, and ALB's subnet requirements](11-networking.md#internal-vs-internet-facing-alb-and-albs-subnet-requirements)** — `internal = true` was **absent from the entire guide**, yet it is the reference topology's whole ingress. Added: what `scheme` actually decides (private-only IPs, DNS resolving to private IPs even from the internet, **no IGW required**) and that it is **immutable after creation**; and — missing guide-wide — **ALB's real subnet requirements: ≥2 AZs, minimum `/27`, and at least 8 free IPs per subnet**, which ties directly to the 5-reserved-IPs rule (a `/28` fails the floor) and to sharing a subnet with `awsvpc` ECS/EKS, where exhausting those 8 IPs makes scale-out fail while presenting as "the ALB is slow." Plus why an internal ALB still needs a private-hosted-zone A-alias, and the two deliberate-but-questionable choices in the reference stack: an HTTP-only listener (with the honest in-VPC-encryption trade-off and the note that ACM public certs work on internal ALBs) and `fixed-response 501` as a default action, which is good design on a shared listener.
4. **[Gap 4 — Interface endpoint AZ/subnet placement, zonal DNS and AZ IDs](11-networking.md#interface-endpoint-azsubnet-placement-zonal-dns-and-az-ids)** — the guide covered interface endpoints' *what*, *why* and cost model but not their **placement mechanics**, which is what makes the reference topology's per-AZ subnet filtering look arbitrary. Added: one subnet per AZ producing one **ENI** each, so three AZs literally means three ENIs and three hourly charges; the three DNS names (regional, **zonal**, private DNS) and what zonal names are actually for; the 1-AZ-vs-every-client-AZ trade-off, where cross-AZ per-GB billed **in both directions** overtakes the hourly saving at any real volume; and the two reasons per-AZ filtering exists — an endpoint can only be created in AZs where the **provider's endpoint service** is available, and, a nuance absent from the whole guide, **AZ *names* map to different physical AZs per account** while **AZ IDs (`use1-az1`) are the stable identifier** you must match on for cross-account and PrivateLink alignment.
5. **[Gap 5 — ECS dynamic host ports, seen from the networking side](11-networking.md#ecs-dynamic-host-ports-seen-from-the-networking-side)** — the mechanics were already well covered in [Containers](09-containers-ecs-fargate.md#ecs-elastic-container-service), but this document's **only** mention of `32768` is the entirely unrelated NACL ephemeral-port trap, and the two get conflated. Added a side-by-side table separating them (where each applies, why it exists, whose traffic, and the distinct symptoms — "timeout where the SG is provably correct" vs "all targets unhealthy"); the reference topology's port-by-port data path showing that the **target group's port is only a placeholder** under `bridge` plus dynamic mapping, which is why `8006` is nowhere in the SG and everything still works; and a `bridge`-vs-`awsvpc` networking comparison whose conclusion connects back to Gap 2 — running `bridge` makes per-service SG isolation *architecturally* impossible regardless of how carefully rules are written, and the `awsvpc` fix costs subnet IP space that, under the platform-owned model, you don't own.

No pre-existing content was removed or rewritten; all five sections are additive and cross-linked back into the existing material.
