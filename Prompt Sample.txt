## Purpose and teaching style

Write explanations, not exercises. Do not create practice tasks, quizzes, coding challenges, step-by-step implementation assignments, or a mini-project for the reader to build. Do not tell the reader to modify code and predict or observe its output.

Teach the topics progressively, from the mental model and fundamentals through implementation details, interactions, edge cases, and design trade-offs. Explain not only what each API does, but why it exists, how the container behaves, when it is appropriate, and what can go wrong. Assume the reader knows basic C# but is new to the deeper mechanics of .NET DI.

Use focused C# code examples to make explanations concrete. Explain each example and its expected behavior in prose; examples are illustrations, not exercises. Keep examples internally consistent, compile-plausible, and specific to .NET 8 unless a version distinction is called out. Distinguish framework guarantees from common implementation behavior and from design recommendations. Do not imply every DI container behaves exactly like the built-in Microsoft container.

## 1. DI mental model and terminology

Explain:

* What dependency injection is and which problems it addresses.
* The relationship and distinctions between IoC, dependency injection, the DI container, a service, a registration, a service descriptor, a service provider, and a scope.
* Composition roots and why registrations are usually made at application startup.
* Constructor injection as the default; method/action injection where ASP.NET Core supports it; property injection and why the built-in container does not provide general property injection.
* How constructor dependencies are discovered and recursively resolved, and what happens for missing registrations, ambiguous constructors, circular dependencies, and invalid dependency graphs.
* The difference between registering a type, registering an instance, and registering a factory delegate.

## 2. Service lifetimes in depth

Explain `Transient`, `Scoped`, and `Singleton` in the built-in .NET container. For each, describe creation, identity reuse, ownership, disposal, appropriate uses, unsuitable uses, and practical examples.

Cover the registration APIs:

```csharp
services.AddTransient<IClock, SystemClock>();
services.AddScoped<IOrderService, OrderService>();
services.AddSingleton<IApplicationCache, ApplicationCache>();
```

Explain precisely:

* A transient is requested/created as a new instance on each resolution, subject to how it is resolved and any containing service.
* A scoped service is reused within one scope, not inherently "one per HTTP request"; an HTTP request normally gets a scope, while manually created scopes have their own scoped instances.
* A singleton is reused by the root provider for the provider's lifetime, and must be designed for concurrent access when used by concurrent requests.
* Disposal ownership and timing for container-created transient, scoped, singleton, and externally supplied instances. Include `IDisposable` and `IAsyncDisposable`, scope disposal, root-provider disposal, and asynchronous disposal.
* What happens when a transient is injected into a singleton, including the fact that the transient may effectively be retained for the singleton's lifetime.
* Why a singleton must not directly capture a scoped dependency; explain captive dependencies, scope validation, and the correct `IServiceScopeFactory` approach when a singleton must perform scoped work.
* Why resolving a scoped service from the root provider is problematic and how development-time validation detects lifetime mismatches.
* The difference between registration lifetime and the lifetime of an object held in a field, cached by application code, or resolved through another service.
* Thread safety, mutable state, `HttpContext`/request data, and safe patterns for each lifetime.

Use concise identity/timeline examples to clarify behavior across two resolutions, two scopes, and two HTTP requests. State what is reused and what is recreated.

## 3. Registration patterns and registration identity

Compare interface-to-implementation, concrete-type, instance, and delegate registrations, including:

```csharp
services.AddTransient<IReportWriter, CsvReportWriter>();
services.AddTransient<CsvReportWriter>();
services.AddSingleton<IClock>(new SystemClock());
services.AddScoped<IReportWriter>(sp => new CsvReportWriter(/* dependencies */));
```

Explain:

* Which service type can be requested for each registration.
* Why registering the same implementation separately for an interface and concrete type can produce different instances, and how to deliberately share one instance across service types.
* How abstraction choices affect coupling, substitutability, testing, and maintainability.
* Multiple registrations for one service type: which registration a single-service resolution returns and how `IEnumerable<T>` behaves.
* Registration order, duplicate registrations, and the difference between `Add...`, `TryAdd...`, and `Replace` where relevant.
* Open generic registrations and representative uses, such as `IRepository<T>`.
* Appropriate use and limitations of factory delegates and prebuilt instances.

## 4. Service provider and resolution APIs

Explain `IServiceProvider`, `IServiceScopeFactory`, `IServiceScope`, and `IServiceProviderIsService`, including their roles and boundaries. Clearly explain:

* `GetService<T>()` / `GetService(typeof(T))` versus `GetRequiredService<T>()` / `GetRequiredService(typeof(T))`.
* Optional resolution versus required resolution, null behavior, and the exception raised when a required service is missing.
* `GetKeyedService<T>()` and `GetRequiredKeyedService<T>()` in .NET 8.
* Why resolving dependencies manually from `IServiceProvider` is called the service locator pattern, why it can obscure dependencies and defer failures, and the limited cases where explicit resolution is appropriate.
* Why application services should usually receive their dependencies through constructors rather than receiving `IServiceProvider`.
* Correct scope creation and disposal with `CreateScope()` and `CreateAsyncScope()`, including resolving scoped services only from the created scope's provider.
* Root provider versus scoped provider, nested scopes (independent scopes rather than hierarchical inheritance), and how a scope is disposed.

Show small examples of optional versus required resolution and manual scope usage, explaining when each is appropriate.

## 5. Factory delegates, factories, and Strategy

Give a precise explanation of the word "factory" in .NET DI and distinguish:

1. A DI registration factory delegate, such as `AddTransient<TService>(sp => ...)`.
2. An application factory abstraction/class that selects or creates a product.
3. The Strategy pattern, where interchangeable behavior is selected behind a common abstraction.
4. `IServiceProvider`-based dynamic resolution and why it should not be the default design.

Explain factory delegates for transient, scoped, and singleton registrations, including how their dependencies are resolved, when the delegate runs, and the lifetime/disposal consequences of returning an instance. Explain when factories are useful (runtime configuration, external resources, conditional construction, or nontrivial creation) and when constructor injection is simpler.

Explain the "strategy with delegate" approach using a typed delegate or a `Func`-style function, how it differs from registering strategy classes, and the trade-offs around readability, testability, lifetime, and selection logic. Do not imply the built-in .NET container automatically creates arbitrary `Func<T>` factories for every registration; describe supported behavior accurately and show an explicit delegate/factory registration where needed.

Compare a factory class, a keyed strategy, an `IEnumerable<TStrategy>` selector, and a delegate. Explain how to keep selection policy in an appropriate application service rather than scattering it through callers.

## 6. Keyed services in .NET 8

Explain the .NET 8 keyed DI feature in detail:

## 9. Common failure modes and design guidance

Explain the cause, symptoms, and appropriate remedy for:

* Captive dependencies and singleton/scoped mismatches.
* Resolving scoped services from the root provider.
* Disposing a dependency manually when the container owns it, or failing to dispose a manually created resource.
* Injecting a transient into a long-lived object and assuming it will be recreated for every operation.
* Constructor cycles, overly large constructors, hidden service-locator dependencies, and registrations that exist under the wrong service type.
* Stateful singletons that are not thread-safe.
* Capturing request-specific data or `HttpContext` in a singleton.
* Overusing keyed services, factories, or DI when a direct dependency or ordinary branching is clearer.

For each, explain the reasoning and a safer design, not just a rule to memorize.
